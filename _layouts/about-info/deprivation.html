Frequently Asked Questions

What Are the Indices of Deprivation?

  • The government's official measure of relative deprivation in England. They rank all {{ dep.lsoa_count_england_display }} small neighbourhoods from most to least deprived, combining seven kinds of deprivation: income, employment, education and skills, health and disability, crime, barriers to housing and services, and living environment.
  • The overall ranking, the Index of Multiple Deprivation, weights income and employment most heavily.

What Is a Neighbourhood Here?

  • A lower layer super output area: a small area the Office for National Statistics draws to hold roughly 1,500 residents. Cheltenham has {{ dep.summary.lsoa_count }} of them, and each ward contains between three and five.

What Do the Deciles Mean?

  • England's neighbourhoods are split into ten equal groups. Decile 1 is the most deprived 10% in the country and decile 10 the least deprived 10%. A neighbourhood in decile 1 for crime, for example, is among the 10% of neighbourhoods in England with the highest levels of crime.

Does It Mean Everyone in a Neighbourhood Is Deprived?

  • No. The indices describe an area as a whole. Plenty of people in a deprived neighbourhood are not deprived, and the reverse is true of less deprived ones.
  • They're relative, too: they show which areas are more deprived than others, not by how much, and not whether an area has got better or worse in absolute terms.

How Is Cheltenham's Overall Rank Worked Out?

  • Districts are ranked by the average deprivation score of their neighbourhoods, weighted by population. Cheltenham's average score of {{ dep.summary.average_score }} puts it {{ dep.summary.rank_of_average_score }}th of {{ dep.summary.district_count }} districts, where 1 is the most deprived.

How Often Are the Figures Updated?

  • Every four to six years. The {{ dep.edition }} edition was published in October 2025; the previous one was published in 2019.
